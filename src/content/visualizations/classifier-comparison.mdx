Every classifier makes assumptions about what a boundary is allowed to look like. That's its
**inductive bias**. It's what lets a model generalize from finite data at all, and it's also what
makes it fail on the wrong problem. The fastest way to understand the classic methods is to run
all of them on the same data and look at what they draw, like scikit-learn's well-known comparison
plot.

## The grid

Each row is one method (click its name for the full interactive page). Each column is one dataset.
Every cell is a model trained just now, with the held-out accuracy in its corner. Hover a cell for
training accuracy and fit time. **New data** draws a fresh sample and retrains all forty.

## Reading it by column

- **Two blobs** is the easy case. Everything gets roughly the same score, and the remaining errors
  are points that sit inside the other class's cloud, which no classifier can fix. When the
  simplest model is as good as the fanciest, use the simplest.
- **Moons** separates the models that can bend from the ones that can't. Logistic regression and
  the linear SVM draw their best straight line and stop in the 80s or low 90s.
- **Circles**: no line gets anywhere near, and the two linear models drop to about coin-flip level.
  Naive Bayes, surprisingly, is fine: per-class Gaussians with different spreads give a closed
  curve for free.
- **XOR** is the interaction test. The linear models fail, and so does Naive Bayes: each feature
  *alone* is identically distributed in both classes, and Naive Bayes only ever looks at features
  alone. A single greedy decision tree can also get stuck on some draws, when no first split has
  any gain (the trap described on the [decision tree](/visualize/decision-tree/) page).
- **Spiral** needs a boundary that winds. The local methods (k-NN, RBF SVM) handle it best. The
  forest does well, and the network gets most of it in its 300 training epochs, while the linear
  models and Naive Bayes barely beat guessing.

## Reading it by row

| Method | Boundary it draws | Training | Prediction | Reach for it when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [k-NN](/visualize/knn/) | anything, locally | none | slow: `O(n)` per query | small data, a meaningful distance |
| [Logistic regression](/visualize/logistic-regression/) | straight line | fast, convex | instant | a baseline, calibrated probabilities, explanations |
| [Linear SVM](/visualize/svm/) | straight line, max margin | fast | instant | high-dimensional sparse data (text) |
| [RBF SVM](/visualize/svm/) | smooth, any shape | `O(n²)`–`O(n³)` | per support vector | small-to-medium data, good features |
| [Naive Bayes](/visualize/naive-bayes/) | axis-aligned conics | one counting pass | instant | text, tiny data, a quick baseline |
| [Decision tree](/visualize/decision-tree/) | axis-aligned staircase | fast | instant | you need to read the model |
| [Random forest](/visualize/random-forest/) | smoothed staircase | parallel trees | per tree | tabular data, little tuning |
| [Neural network](/visualize/neural-network/) | anything, learned | slowest, non-convex | instant | lots of data, images/text/audio ([CNN](/visualize/cnn/)) |

Look along the rows for signatures. The tree's boundaries are always horizontal and vertical
steps, even on the circles. The forest's are the same steps, averaged into something smoother.
k-NN's regions wrap around individual points. The SVM's and the network's are smooth curves.

## No free lunch

No row wins every column, and that's not a flaw of these particular datasets. The **no free lunch
theorem** (Wolpert, 1996) says that averaged over *all* possible problems, every classifier does
equally well. A method is only better *for a kind of problem*, because its assumptions match that
kind. The practical version:

- Try the simple model first. If logistic regression is within a point of everything else, ship
  it.
- On **tabular** data, gradient-boosted trees and random forests are the ones to beat.
- On **images, audio and text**, learned representations (CNNs, transformers) win by a wide
  margin, because a hand-built feature space can't compete with one learned from millions of
  examples.

## Take the numbers with a pinch of salt

Each cell is one random split of 160 points, with a 48-point test set, so a single test point is
worth about 2%. Press **New data** a few times: a difference of a couple of points between two
methods on one draw means nothing. Real comparisons use cross-validation over several splits.
Every model here also uses fixed, reasonable settings; tuning `k`, `C`, `γ` or depth per dataset
would move several cells.
