Handwritten digits were *the* pattern-recognition benchmark for decades, and convolutional
networks are what cracked them. LeCun's LeNet was reading the amounts on bank cheques in the
1990s. The network on this page is a miniature of the same design: small enough to see every
number it computes, and still accurate.

```
16×16 image → conv, 8 filters of 3×3 → ReLU → 2×2 max-pool → dense 392 → 10 → softmax
```

## Try it

Draw a digit in the box, with a mouse, finger or pen. The network sees your drawing cropped,
scaled into a 12×12 box and centred in 16×16, the same preparation MNIST used. Each of the eight
cards below is one learned filter: its 3×3 weights, the **feature map** you get by sliding it over
the image, and that map after max-pooling. The ten bars are the output.

## Why not a plain neural network?

A [fully connected network](/visualize/neural-network/) could read these images too. Flatten the
256 pixels and feed them in. But it would learn "a vertical stroke at column 7" and "a vertical
stroke at column 8" as unrelated facts, each with its own weights. A convolution layer builds in
two things we already know about images:

- **Locality**: a 3×3 filter looks at a small neighbourhood, because nearby pixels are what make up
  strokes and edges.
- **Weight sharing**: the same 9 weights are used at every position, so a stroke detector works
  wherever the stroke is.

The whole convolution layer is 80 parameters, 8 × (9 weights + 1 bias). The detail of how one
filter slides over an image is on the [convolution & pooling](/visualize/convolution/) page. Here
the filters aren't hand-designed, they're learned.

## What the filters learn

Look at the eight kernels. They're messier than textbook filters like Sobel, but the patterns are
there. In the shipped weights, filter 2 is positive across the top row and strongly negative at the
bottom, so it fires along the lower edge of a horizontal stroke. Filter 4 weighs its left columns
against its right one, which makes it a vertical-edge detector. Others are mostly negative with a
positive bias: they respond to *empty paper*, which is information too, since where the ink isn't
separates a 0 from an 8. Draw a 7 and compare maps: different filters light up on the bar and on
the diagonal.

After ReLU, **max-pooling** keeps the strongest response in each 2×2 block. The map halves in size
and a one-pixel shift of the stroke often doesn't change it at all, which gives the network a
little tolerance to exactly where you drew.

The dense layer then reads all 8 × 7 × 7 = 392 pooled values and scores each digit, like a
logistic regression over detected strokes. Of the model's 4,010 parameters, 3,930 are here.

## Watch it learn

**Retrain from scratch** throws the weights away and trains a new network in your browser: 8,000
generated digits, 5 passes, Adam — a few seconds on a laptop. The filters start as random noise
and sharpen into stroke detectors as the loss drops. Run it twice. Different random starts give
*different* filters and about the same accuracy. There are many equally good ways to split the
work between eight detectors.

## Where the training data comes from

There's no dataset download here. The digits are **generated**: each is a few pen strokes (a
couple of common ways of writing each digit), bent by a random rotation, slant, stretch and
wobble, then drawn at a random pen width. The held-out accuracy is measured on 500 more of them,
from a different random seed and with extra distortion.

That's also the honest limit of this demo. The network has only seen *this generator's*
handwriting. It reads normal digits well, but an unusual style it never saw (a heavily slanted 1, or a 9 with a curled
tail) can fool it. This is
**distribution shift**, the same thing that trips up much larger models when real-world data
doesn't look like their training set.

## Scaling up

Real vision networks stack this block many times. Each layer's filters work on the previous
layer's feature maps rather than on pixels, so the **receptive field** grows. The first layer sees
3×3 pixels, the next effectively 5×5 or more, and a few layers in, a single unit responds to a
whole digit part or, in a photo, a whole eye. AlexNet (2012) did this at scale on ImageNet with
GPUs, and set off the deep-learning era. Vision transformers have since replaced the fixed local
window with [attention](/visualize/self-attention/), but convolutions are still everywhere
cameras are.
