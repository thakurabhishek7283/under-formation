A decision tree learns by asking one question at a time: *is x below 42?* Each answer sends you to
a smaller sub-problem, solved the same way. There is no gradient, no loss surface and no
initialization — just a search over thresholds, repeated.

The interesting part isn't the tree, it's the *score*: how do you decide which question is best?

## Try it

Step through the growth. At each node you'll see the curve of **impurity gain** for every candidate
threshold, drawn against the axis it splits on — below the plot for `x`, beside it for `y`. The
tree takes the peak. The dashed box is the region the current node is responsible for.

## Impurity: how mixed is this node?

A node holding 20 of class A and 0 of class B is **pure** — nothing to decide. A node holding 10
and 10 is as impure as it gets. Two ways to put a number on that, both zero when pure and maximal
at 50/50:

```
Gini(p)    = 1 − Σ pᵢ²           # chance of mislabelling a random sample
Entropy(p) = − Σ pᵢ log₂ pᵢ      # bits needed to describe the label
```

For a 50/50 node, Gini = 0.5 and entropy = 1 bit. The two rarely disagree about which split is
best — switch the control and watch the chosen cuts barely move. Gini is slightly cheaper (no
logarithm), which is why it's the scikit-learn default; entropy has the nicer interpretation, since
the gain is then literally **information gain**, the bits of label uncertainty the question
removes.

## The gain of a split

Score a candidate by how much impurity it removes, weighting each side by how many samples land
there:

```
gain = I(parent) − (n_left/n)·I(left) − (n_right/n)·I(right)
```

The weighting matters. A split that peels off 3 perfectly pure samples and leaves 97 untouched
barely helps, even though one side is spotless. The tree tries every midpoint between consecutive
values on every feature — a few hundred candidates per node — and keeps the best. That's `O(n·d)`
per node with sorted features, which is why trees train so fast.

## Greedy, and what that costs

The tree never reconsiders. It takes the locally best cut and moves on, which is exactly why the
**XOR** dataset is so instructive: choose it and look at the gain curve on the first node. It's
flat — *no* single cut on either feature separates anything, because each half of `x` still holds
an even mix of both classes. A greedy learner that demanded positive gain would give up here. The
tree survives only because it splits anyway and finds real gain one level down.

That's the general shape of the problem: trees can't see combinations of features until they've
already committed to one of them. It's also why an axis-aligned tree is so awkward on the
**Diagonal** data — a 45° boundary that one linear model gets exactly right costs the tree a long
staircase of cuts, and it still only approximates it.

## Depth, and the moment it turns into memorizing

Drag **max depth** up and watch the two accuracy curves separate. Training accuracy climbs towards
100% — given enough depth every training point gets its own leaf, and a leaf containing one point
is never wrong about it. Held-out accuracy peaks somewhere in the middle and then falls. The gap
between the curves *is* overfitting, and the data has 6% label noise in it precisely so you can see
the tree dutifully learning the noise.

Ways to stop it, all visible in the controls or one step away:

- **Max depth** — a blunt cap.
- **Min samples per split** — refuse to split a node that's already small, where "the best cut"
  is just noise. Raise it and the deep-tree collapse softens.
- **Pruning** — grow the full tree, then cut back the branches that don't pay for themselves on
  held-out data (cost-complexity pruning). Usually better than a depth cap, because different
  branches deserve different depths.

## Why forests

One deep tree is a high-variance model: change a few training points and you get a visibly
different tree. That's a weakness you can average away. **Random forests** train many trees, each
on a bootstrap sample of the rows and each split choosing from a random subset of features, then
vote. The individual trees stay deep and overfit-y; their *errors* are decorrelated enough that the
average isn't.

**Gradient boosting** (XGBoost, LightGBM) goes the other way: many deliberately shallow trees, each
fitted to the residual error left by the ones before it. On tabular data, boosted trees are still
the model to beat — often ahead of neural networks.
