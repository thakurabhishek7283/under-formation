Give k-means a pile of unlabelled points and a number `k`, and it will hand back `k` groups. It is
the first unsupervised algorithm most people meet, and it is built from two steps so simple they
look like they couldn't possibly be enough:

1. **Assign** each point to the nearest centre.
2. **Move** each centre to the mean of the points that chose it.

Repeat until nothing changes. That's the whole algorithm — Lloyd's algorithm, 1957.

## Try it

Step through with ▶ and watch the two half-steps alternate. The shaded regions are the **Voronoi
cells** of the centres: the assign step draws exactly these boundaries, because "nearest centre"
*is* the cell you fall in. Points that switch cluster are outlined and drawn with a line to their
new centre.

## Why it converges

Both half-steps minimize the same quantity, the **inertia** (within-cluster sum of squares):

```
J = Σᵢ ‖xᵢ − c_{a(i)}‖²        a(i) = the cluster point i is assigned to
```

- The **assign** step holds the centres fixed and picks, for each point, the `a(i)` with the
  smallest distance. It cannot raise `J`.
- The **update** step holds the assignment fixed and replaces each centre with its cluster's mean.
  The mean is precisely the point that minimizes the sum of squared distances to a set, so this
  cannot raise `J` either.

`J` never increases, and there are only finitely many possible assignments, so the algorithm must
stop. Watch the inertia chart: it steps down twice per iteration and then flattens.

That argument guarantees convergence — **it does not guarantee a good answer**. It stops at a local
optimum. Press **Restart** a few times on *Uneven sizes*: the same data, the same `k`, different
starting centres, different final clustering.

## Why the start matters, and k-means++

With random starting points, two centres can land inside one true cluster, and the algorithm will
happily split that cluster in half while lumping two others together — nothing in the update rule
can undo it.

**k-means++** fixes this cheaply. The first centre is a random point; each later one is drawn with
probability proportional to **D²**, its squared distance to the nearest centre chosen so far. Far
away points are far more likely to be picked, so the starting centres spread out. Switch the
**Start** control to `Random points` and step through the init phase both ways — under k-means++
the shading shows the D² weights each centre is drawn from.

In practice k-means++ both converges in fewer iterations and lands in better optima often enough
that it's the default in scikit-learn.

## Choosing k

k-means cannot tell you `k`; you have to. Inertia is no help on its own, because it *always* falls
as `k` rises — at `k = n` every point is its own cluster and `J = 0`. The usual move is to look for
the **elbow**: the `k` after which the curve flattens out. On the three-blob data the bend at
`k = 3` is obvious; on the moons there is no bend at all, which is itself informative.

More principled alternatives: the **silhouette score** (how much closer a point is to its own
cluster than to the next nearest), or the **gap statistic** (compare inertia to what you'd get on
uniform random data).

## What it can't do

The failure cases in the picker aren't edge cases, they're the assumptions showing through:

| Dataset | What happens | The assumption it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Uneven sizes | The big loose cluster gets split | Clusters have similar spread |
| Stretched | Cuts *across* the elongated clusters | Distance is isotropic — no notion of shape |
| Two moons | Never recovers the moons at any `k` | Clusters are convex blobs around a centre |

k-means draws straight boundaries halfway between centres, because that's what "nearest centre"
means. If your clusters aren't roughly round and roughly equal, the boundary it can draw is the
wrong shape. The fixes are different algorithms, not more iterations: **Gaussian mixtures** with
full covariance for stretched clusters, **DBSCAN** for arbitrary shapes and noise, or **spectral
clustering**, which embeds the points first and then runs k-means in a space where the moons *are*
separable.

## Cost

Each iteration is `O(n · k · d)` — every point against every centre. That's cheap, and it's why
k-means still gets used on very large datasets when better-fitting methods would be too slow.
**Mini-batch k-means** samples a batch per iteration instead of the full set and gives up a little
quality for a large speedup.
