Most classifiers learn a compact model from the data and then throw the data away. k-nearest
neighbours does the opposite: it keeps every training example and does all its work when asked
a question. To classify a new point, find the `k` stored examples closest to it and let them vote.

That's the entire algorithm (Fix & Hodges, 1951). It has no parameters to fit, no loss function
and no training loop, and it is still a strong baseline on many problems.

## k is the whole bias–variance trade-off

With **k = 1** the boundary follows every single training point. Each noisy sample gets its own
little island, and training accuracy is always 100% — every point is its own nearest neighbour,
so "training accuracy" measures nothing. As **k** grows the vote averages over a wider
neighbourhood: the boundary smooths out and islands disappear. Push k far enough and it
over-smooths, until every region votes for whichever class has more points overall.

The accuracy chart shows both failure modes. On the overlapping **Two blobs**, k = 1 trails the best k by a few
points on held-out data: it has memorized noise. On the **Spiral**, large k is a
disaster. Once the neighbourhood is wider than the gap between the arms, it takes in both
classes, and held-out accuracy sinks towards a coin flip. Choose k by cross-validation, and for two
classes use an odd k so the vote can't tie.

There's a neat guarantee at the k = 1 end. With enough data, the 1-NN error rate is at most twice
the **Bayes error** — the error of the best possible classifier (Cover & Hart, 1967). Half of all
the information needed to classify is in the single nearest neighbour.

## What "near" means is a modelling decision

Every choice of distance is a claim about which examples should count as similar:

| Metric | Formula | Neighbourhood |
|---|---|---|
| Euclidean (L2) | `√(dx² + dy²)` | circle |
| Manhattan (L1) | `\|dx\| + \|dy\|` | diamond |
| Chebyshev (L∞) | `max(\|dx\|, \|dy\|)` | square |

On these 2-D toy sets the three give similar answers. With real features they can differ a lot,
and domain-specific distances (edit distance for strings, cosine similarity for text embeddings)
are often what make k-NN work at all.

## Standardize your features, or one of them wins by default

Tick **Forget to standardize**. Now x is measured in units 5× smaller than y, as if one feature
were in millimetres and the other in metres. The neighbourhood collapses into a tall thin sliver:
a small change in x now outweighs a large change in y, so the vote is decided almost entirely by
x, and the regions turn into vertical stripes.

Nothing about the data changed, only its units. That's why distance-based methods (k-NN, k-means,
SVMs with an RBF kernel) almost always start by rescaling each feature to zero mean and unit
variance.

## Weighted votes

With **1/distance** weighting, closer neighbours count for more. It softens the effect of k —
far-away members of the neighbourhood barely matter — and it makes ties vanishingly unlikely.
It also makes a query that lands exactly on a training point return that point's label, like
k = 1.

## The catch: cost, and high dimensions

k-NN moves all the cost to prediction time. One query means one distance per stored example —
the stat tile counts them — so it's `O(n · d)` per prediction, and memory grows with the dataset.
Spatial indexes (KD-trees, ball trees) help in low dimensions. At scale, **approximate** nearest
neighbour search (HNSW graphs, product quantization) trades a little accuracy for orders of
magnitude of speed. That's the same machinery behind vector databases and retrieval for LLMs,
which are nearest-neighbour lookups over embeddings.

The deeper problem is the **curse of dimensionality**. In hundreds of dimensions, the nearest and
farthest points in a random dataset are almost the same distance away, so "nearest" stops meaning
"similar". k-NN works when a good representation makes distance meaningful. That's why it pairs
so well with learned embeddings, and why it struggles on raw high-dimensional inputs.
