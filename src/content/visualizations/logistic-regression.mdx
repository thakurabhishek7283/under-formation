The simplest useful classifier computes a weighted sum of the features and checks its sign:

```
score = w₀ + w₁·x + w₂·y          predict B if score > 0, else A
```

The boundary is the line `score = 0`, and the weight vector `(w₁, w₂)` points across it towards
class B. Every model on this page draws that kind of line. They differ only in *how they choose
w*, and that choice turns out to matter a great deal.

## Try it

Pick a method and step through its training. For the perceptron, each step is one correction:
the ringed point was on the wrong side, and the dashed line is where the boundary was before it
got fixed. For logistic regression, each step is one full gradient-descent epoch, and the
background shading is the predicted probability.

## The perceptron: learn only from mistakes

Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958) was the first learning machine built in hardware. Its rule is
almost too simple to believe:

```
if y · (w · x) ≤ 0:        # sample on the wrong side  (y is +1 or −1)
    w ← w + y · x          # add it to the weights
```

Adding a misclassified sample tilts the line towards classifying that sample correctly. Correct
samples change nothing. On the **Separable** data it stops after a handful of corrections, and
the **perceptron convergence theorem** (Novikoff, 1962) guarantees it always will. If every
sample lies within radius `R` and some line separates the classes with margin `γ`, it makes at
most `(R/γ)²` mistakes, however the data is ordered.

The two weaknesses are visible too:

- **It stops at the first line that works**, not the best one. Nothing in the rule prefers a line
  far from both classes over one that grazes a point. That missing preference is exactly what
  [support vector machines](/visualize/svm/) add.
- **It never stops if no line exists.** Switch to *Overlapping blobs*: some point is always on the
  wrong side, so the weights keep lurching back and forth until the step cap.

## Logistic regression: a probability, and a gradient

Logistic regression keeps the same score but passes it through the **sigmoid**,
`σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e⁻ᶻ)`, to get a probability: `P(B | x) = σ(w · x)`. Far on the B side the
probability is near 1, far on the A side near 0, and exactly 0.5 on the line. The panel under
the weights shows this directly: every training sample, placed at its score on the sigmoid.

Training minimizes the **log loss**, the average of `−log P(true class)`. Its gradient has a
familiar shape:

```
∇ = (1/n) · Σ (p − y) · x
```

It's the perceptron update, except that every sample takes part, weighted by *how wrong* it is
(`p − y`), rather than only the misclassified ones counting, each at full strength. A point on
the right side but close to the line still pulls a little, so the line drifts towards the middle
of the gap. The log loss is **convex**, so gradient descent can't get stuck: there's one bowl and
it always finds the bottom.

## Why the weights keep growing

On the **Separable** data, watch `|w|` in the stats while the loss creeps towards zero. When the
classes can be separated perfectly, the loss is minimized by being *infinitely confident*: scale
`w` up and every probability moves closer to 0 or 1. There's no finite optimum, and the sigmoid
sharpens into a step.

An **L2 penalty** `λ‖w‖²` fixes this. Raise λ and the weights settle at a finite size. The
probabilities stay honest near the boundary, and the model generalizes better on noisy data. In
practice logistic regression is almost always regularized (scikit-learn does it by default).

## Straight lines in a curved space

On **Circles** or **XOR**, no line works, and both methods fail. Switch the features to
*quadratic*. The model now scores `w · (1, x, y, x², y², xy)`. It is still linear in its weights,
trained exactly the same way, but the boundary it draws in the plane is now a curve: an ellipse
for the circles, and for XOR a hyperbola hugging the axes (the `xy` term alone is positive in two
opposite quadrants and negative in the other two, which is XOR exactly).

That idea — **transform the inputs, then fit a linear model** — is the thread through the rest of
classical ML:

- SVMs do it implicitly with [kernels](/visualize/svm/), without ever computing the new features.
- [Neural networks](/visualize/neural-network/) *learn* the transformation instead of having it
  hand-written. A network with no hidden layer is exactly this model.

It's also why this model mattered historically. Minsky & Papert's *Perceptrons* (1969) proved
that a single-layer perceptron can't compute XOR. The fix, hidden layers that learn their own
features, needed backpropagation to train, and that didn't catch on until the 1980s.

## More than two classes

Replace the sigmoid with a **softmax** over one score per class and you have multinomial logistic
regression, the output layer of nearly every neural classifier. It remains the default first
model for tabular problems: fast, convex, well-calibrated probabilities, and weights you can
read.
