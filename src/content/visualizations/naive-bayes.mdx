The other classifiers in this series learn the **boundary** directly — where class A ends and B
begins. A **generative** classifier asks a different question: *what does each class look like?*
It fits a probability model `p(x | class)` to each class separately, then uses Bayes' rule to turn
those models around:

```
P(class | x)  =  P(class) · p(x | class)  /  p(x)
```

Prior times likelihood, normalized. The class with the largest product wins, and the decision
boundary is simply wherever two products are equal.

## Try it

Each class gets a 2-D Gaussian, fitted by nothing more than averaging: a mean (the `+`) and a
covariance (the ellipses at 1σ and 2σ). The table works through Bayes' rule at the probe point.
Drag to move the probe and watch the likelihoods trade places across the boundary.

## Three models, one knob

The three buttons differ only in what shape the covariance is allowed to have:

| Model | Covariance | Boundary | Parameters (d features, K classes) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naive Bayes | diagonal, one per class | quadratic, axis-aligned | `2dK` |
| LDA | full, **shared** by all classes | straight line | `dK + d(d+1)/2` |
| QDA | full, one per class | any conic | `dK + K·d(d+1)/2` |

(plus the `K − 1` class priors.) Why does sharing a covariance make the boundary straight?
Taking logs of Bayes' rule, each class contributes a quadratic term `−½ xᵀΣ⁻¹x`. If every class
uses the same Σ, that term is identical on both sides of the comparison and cancels, leaving
something linear in `x`. That's **Linear Discriminant Analysis**, from R. A. Fisher's 1936 paper
on iris flowers. It's one of the oldest pattern-recognition methods still in daily use.

Give each class its own Σ and the quadratic terms no longer cancel: **QDA** can draw ellipses and
hyperbolas.

## The naive assumption

Naive Bayes assumes the features are **independent given the class**, so the joint likelihood
factors into a product of one-dimensional pieces:

```
p(x, y | class) = p(x | class) · p(y | class)
```

The two small plots beside the map are those pieces. The probe's height on each curve is the
factor that goes into the table.

Now switch to the **Correlated** data. Each class is a long diagonal cloud, and the two clouds sit
side by side. Look at the marginal plots: along x alone, and along y alone, the two classes look
almost *identical*. All the information is in the correlation — "high x goes with high y, but
class B is shifted" — and that's precisely what Naive Bayes throws away. On the default
sample it gets only about two in three right, while LDA, which models the tilt, gets well over 90%. On **Circles** the roles reverse:
LDA can only draw a line and does little better than a coin flip, while Naive Bayes and QDA both find the ring.

## Why assume something so wrong?

Because the full covariance costs `d²` parameters, and the diagonal costs `d`. With two features
it hardly matters. With 50,000 features — one per word in a vocabulary — a full covariance has over
a billion entries and no dataset could estimate it. Naive Bayes needs one number per feature per
class, trains in a single counting pass, and its *rankings* are often right even when its
probabilities are badly overconfident (Domingos & Pazzani, 1997). That's why it became the classic
text classifier.

## The spam filter

For text, each class gets a distribution over words instead of a Gaussian: `P(word | spam)` is how
often that word appears in spam, out of all spam words. A message is scored by adding each word's
**log-likelihood ratio** to the prior log-odds. Working in logs turns a product of tiny
probabilities into a readable sum:

Things to try:

- *Free lunch at the meeting tomorrow*: "free" argues hard for spam, but "lunch", "meeting" and
  "tomorrow" outvote it. Each word is judged alone, and the evidence simply adds up.
- **Set α to 0.** Smoothing off, the probability of a word never seen in one class is exactly
  zero, so one unseen word makes that class *impossible*, whatever the rest of the message says.
  **Laplace smoothing** pretends every word was seen `α` extra times in every class, which keeps
  every probability positive.
- Reorder the words. Nothing changes: this is a **bag of words**, and grammar is invisible to it.

This is the design Paul Graham popularized in *A Plan for Spam* (2002), and word-counting Bayesian
filters were the backbone of spam filtering for years afterwards.

## Generative vs discriminative

Logistic regression and LDA can draw exactly the same family of boundaries (straight lines), but
they fit them differently. LDA models where the data comes from; logistic regression models only
the boundary. When the Gaussian assumption holds, LDA uses the data more efficiently and learns
from fewer examples. When it doesn't, logistic regression's weaker assumptions win as the data
grows (Ng & Jordan, 2001). Generative models have one more trick: since they model `p(x | class)`,
they can notice when an input looks like *no* class at all.
