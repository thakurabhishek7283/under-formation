An artificial neural network — in its classic form, a **multi-layer perceptron** — is what you
get when [logistic regression](/visualize/logistic-regression/) stops being handed its features
and starts learning them. Each neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs and passes it through
a non-linearity:

```
a = act(w · inputs + b)
```

A layer is many of those side by side, and a network is layers in sequence. The last layer is a
softmax over the classes, so the whole network ends in a logistic regression whose inputs are the
features the hidden layers built.

## Try it

Press **Train**. Every tile in the network shows what that one neuron outputs at every point of the
plane. The input tiles are just x and y. Each first-layer neuron is one soft straight cut. Deeper
neurons combine the cuts of the layer before. Lines are weights: blue positive, red negative,
thicker for larger magnitude.

## Watching it build a boundary

Start on **Circles** with one hidden layer:

- **0 hidden layers**: plain logistic regression. One straight line, and the circles stay at
  about 50%.
- **1 neuron**: one soft cut. It can't enclose anything.
- **2 neurons**: two cuts, which can carve out a band but not a closed region.
- **3 neurons**: three cuts make a triangle, and the output layer can now say "inside all three".
  Accuracy jumps to nearly 100%.
- **4 or more**: the polygon gets more sides and the boundary rounder.

Look at the first-layer tiles after training. Each is a half-plane, dark on one side and light on
the other, at a different angle. The output neuron's job is to combine them, and that's the
general pattern: early layers compute simple features, later layers combine them into complex ones.

The **universal approximation theorem** (Cybenko, 1989; Hornik, 1991) says one hidden layer
with enough neurons can approximate any continuous function. It doesn't say how many neurons, or
that gradient descent will find the weights. In practice **depth** is the cheaper way to buy
flexibility: each layer can fold the space the previous one produced. Try the **Spiral** with one
wide layer, then with three layers.

## Activations

Without a non-linearity, stacking layers achieves nothing: a linear function of a linear function
is still linear, and the network collapses back into logistic regression.

- **sigmoid** squashes to (0, 1). It was the historical default, but its gradient is at most 0.25 and
  vanishes when saturated, so deep sigmoid networks train slowly.
- **tanh** is a rescaled sigmoid centred on 0, which usually trains faster.
- **ReLU**, `max(0, z)`, is the modern default: cheap, and it doesn't saturate for positive inputs.
  Every ReLU network is piecewise linear, so its boundaries are made of straight segments. Switch
  to ReLU on the circles and the smooth ring becomes a polygon.

## How it learns

Every weight is adjusted by gradient descent on the cross-entropy loss. The gradient for every
weight in every layer comes from one backward pass of the chain rule — that's
[backpropagation](/visualize/backpropagation/). The update rule here is Adam (see
[optimizers](/visualize/gradient-descent/)), applied after each mini-batch of 10 samples.

Unlike the logistic-regression loss, a network's loss surface is **not convex**. Different starting
weights can end in different solutions, and occasionally in poor ones: if a spiral run stalls,
press **New weights** and train again. ReLU networks with a high learning rate are especially
prone to this: a large update can push a neuron to output 0 for every input, and a "dead" ReLU
gets no gradient to recover. The opposite failure is a blow-up: set the learning rate to 0.1 on a
deep network and the loss curve can jump back up after it has already fallen. The steps are
too big, and the tanh neurons slam into saturation.

## Overfitting, live

Give the **Spiral** 3 layers of 8 tanh neurons and let it run for a thousand epochs or so.
Training accuracy reaches 100% and training loss heads to zero, while the held-out loss, after
first falling, turns around and climbs. The network is becoming
**confidently wrong** on the points it never saw. Its boundary threads through individual training
points. With ~180 parameters and ~140 training points, it can afford to memorize. The standard
defences are more data, weight decay, dropout, and stopping when the held-out curve bottoms out
(early stopping).

## From here to deep learning

This is the same architecture as every deep network, just very small. Scale it up and change the
wiring to suit the data: [convolutions](/visualize/cnn/) that share weights across an image,
[attention](/visualize/self-attention/) that routes information between tokens. The training
loop — forward, loss, backward, update — doesn't change.
