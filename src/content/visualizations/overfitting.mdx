Every supervised model has a dial for how flexible it is: polynomial degree here, tree depth in a
[decision tree](/visualize/decision-tree/), width and depth in a network. Turn it up and training
error falls, always, all the way to zero. Test error does something else — it falls, bottoms out,
and then climbs. Everything about model selection follows from that one shape.

## Try it

Start at degree 9 with no penalty and press **New sample** a few times. The fit lurches around
wildly between samples while the training points are matched almost exactly. Then switch the
penalty to **L2** and raise λ, and watch the curve calm down.

## Fitting the noise

The model can only see the training points, and those points are *signal plus noise*. A
low-degree polynomial isn't flexible enough to chase the noise, so it can't. A degree-12
polynomial through 15 points has more than enough freedom to pass near all of them, and the only
way to do that is to bend towards each one's individual error.

The giveaway is the weight chart. Fit degree 10+ with no penalty and look at the largest |w|:
often in the hundreds or thousands. Those huge coefficients are fighting each other — a big
positive `x⁸` term cancelled by a big negative `x⁹` term — and the cancellation is exact only near
the training points. Between them, nothing holds it together, and the curve flies off.

Two things fix this without changing the model class at all: **more data** (raise the training
points slider at high degree and watch the wiggling subside — the same model becomes well behaved
once there are enough constraints) and **regularization**.

## Bias and variance

Tick **Refit on 24 fresh samples**. Each faint orange curve is the same model trained on a
different draw from the same process; the dashed line is their average. Two independent things can
go wrong:

- **Bias²** — how far the *average* fit sits from the true function. At degree 1 the average is a
  straight line through a sine wave: badly wrong no matter how much data you collect.
- **Variance** — how much the individual fits differ from that average. At degree 12 the curves
  fan out enormously: each one is chasing its own sample's noise.

Expected test error decomposes exactly into the two, plus the noise you can never remove:

```
E[(y − f̂(x))²] = Bias[f̂(x)]² + Var[f̂(x)] + σ²
                   ↑ too rigid   ↑ too eager   ↑ irreducible
```

Step the degree up from 0 and watch the numbers trade off: bias falls, variance rises. The best
model is not the one that minimizes either — it's the one that minimizes the sum, which is what
the bottom of the test-error curve is.

This is also why "more flexible model" is not the same as "better model", and why a model that
fits your training data perfectly should worry you rather than please you.

## What the penalties actually do

Both penalties add a cost for large weights, so the fit has to justify every bit of wiggle it
buys:

```
L2 (ridge):  minimize  ‖y − Xw‖² + λ‖w‖²      ⟶  w = (XᵀX + λI)⁻¹Xᵀy
L1 (lasso):  minimize  ‖y − Xw‖² + λ‖w‖₁      ⟶  no closed form
```

**Ridge** shrinks everything smoothly and never quite to zero. Its gradient contribution is `2λw`,
which gets weaker as `w` approaches zero — so it pushes hard on the huge coefficients that cause
wild curves and barely touches the small ones. Adding `λI` makes the matrix well-conditioned too,
which is why ridge is the standard fix for correlated features.

**Lasso** shrinks by a constant amount regardless of size, so weights that aren't earning their
keep get pushed to *exactly* zero and stay there. Switch to L1, raise λ, and watch the non-zero
weight count drop — the model is selecting features, not just damping them. That's the reason to
reach for L1: you get a sparse, readable model.

The λ dial spans the whole story: at 0 you have the unregularized fit; too large and everything is
shrunk towards a flat line, which is bias again. λ is chosen by cross-validation, not by eye.

## Where this shows up in deep learning

The same ideas, renamed:

- **Weight decay** is L2, and it's in nearly every optimizer used to train large models — AdamW is
  literally [Adam](/visualize/gradient-descent/) with weight decay decoupled from the gradient.
- **Early stopping** works because training and validation error separate over *time* as well as
  over model size. Stopping at the validation minimum is a regularizer you get for free.
- **Dropout** and **data augmentation** attack variance directly, by making it harder for the
  network to rely on any one path or any one memorized example.

The modern wrinkle: very large networks often *don't* follow the U-curve. Past the point of
interpolating the training set, test error can fall again — **double descent**. The bias-variance
tradeoff still holds for a fixed model class, but "more parameters is worse" stopped being a
reliable rule once models got big enough to have many exact fits and an optimizer with a taste for
the smooth ones.
