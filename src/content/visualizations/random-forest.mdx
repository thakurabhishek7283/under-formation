A deep [decision tree](/visualize/decision-tree/) fits its training data almost perfectly and
generalizes badly. Change a few training points and you get a visibly different tree: low bias,
high variance. Ensembles turn that weakness into a strength. If every tree makes *different*
mistakes, a vote among many of them cancels the mistakes and keeps what they agree on.

There are two ways to get many different trees. **Bagging** and **random forests** train them
independently on perturbed data and average them. **Boosting** trains them in sequence, each one
aimed at what the ensemble still gets wrong.

## Bagging: same learner, resampled data

**B**ootstrap **agg**regat**ing** (Breiman, 1996): draw `n` rows *with replacement* from the `n`
training rows, fit a deep tree to that sample, repeat, and average the trees' votes. Each bootstrap
sample contains about 63% of the distinct rows (1 − 1/e), with some repeated. Every tree therefore
sees a slightly different dataset and grows differently. Look at the small maps: same data, eight
different jagged partitions.

The ~37% of rows a tree *didn't* see are a free validation set. The **out-of-bag error** under
each small map scores the tree on exactly those rows, so a bagged model estimates its own test
error without a held-out split.

## Random forests: decorrelate the trees

Averaging `B` models with variance `σ²` and pairwise correlation `ρ` leaves a variance of

```
ρ·σ² + (1 − ρ)·σ²/B
```

More trees shrink the second term to nothing, but the first term stays, however many you add.
The trees in a bagged ensemble are still quite correlated, because they tend to make the same
strong first splits. **Random forests** (Breiman, 2001) attack `ρ` directly: at every split, a
tree may only choose among a random subset of features. With two features, the forest here lets
each split see just one of them, picked at random.

Each individual tree usually gets *worse*. On most of these datasets, XOR and Circles especially,
the forest's trees have visibly higher out-of-bag errors than the bagged ones. But the trees
disagree more, so the average improves. It's the central paradox of ensembles: weaker, more
diverse members can make a stronger committee.

Notice what the accuracy chart does *not* do. Adding trees never makes a forest overfit. The
held-out curve rises and then flattens, because more trees just give a better estimate of the same
average. What overfits is depth — try depth 12 against depth 3.

## AdaBoost: fix what's still wrong

Boosting runs the other way round. Start with **stumps**, trees with a single split, which on
their own are weak learners: on hard data, not much better than guessing. Then (Freund & Schapire, 1997):

1. Train a stump on the weighted data.
2. Measure its weighted error `ε` and give it a vote `α = ln((1 − ε)/ε)`. Better stumps get a
   bigger say.
3. **Increase the weight** of every point it got wrong, renormalize, and go back to step 1.

In AdaBoost mode the small maps show the weights as point sizes. Stump #1 sees every point at the
same size. By stump #3 the points near the boundary, the ones the earlier stumps kept missing, have
swollen, and the later stumps concentrate on them. The ensemble is a weighted vote of stumps, so its
boundary is built from axis-aligned steps. With enough of them it approximates any shape.

Now try **XOR** with depth 1 and watch AdaBoost stall. A sum of stumps is an *additive* model —
some function of x plus some function of y — and XOR is pure interaction: neither feature means
anything without the other. Raise the depth to 2 so each learner can capture one interaction, and
held-out accuracy climbs past 90%.

## Gradient boosting: the modern descendant

AdaBoost turns out to be gradient descent on an exponential loss, one tree per step. **Gradient
boosting** (Friedman, 2001) makes that explicit and works with any loss: each new tree is fitted
to the gradient of the loss with respect to the current predictions, usually with a small learning
rate. Its engineered descendants, XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost, remain the strongest
general-purpose models for **tabular data**, frequently ahead of neural networks. On spreadsheets
and database tables, the default choice is still a forest of trees.
