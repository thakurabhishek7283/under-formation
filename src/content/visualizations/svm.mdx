When two classes can be separated by a line, infinitely many lines do it. The
[perceptron](/visualize/logistic-regression/) takes whichever one it reaches first. A support
vector machine asks for the best one: the line that stays as far as possible from both classes,
the one with the widest **margin**.

That single preference leads to three of the best ideas in classical machine learning: a model
that depends on only a handful of training points, a clean way to trade margin for mistakes, and
the kernel trick.

## Try it

The solid line is the decision boundary `f(x) = 0`. The dashed lines are the margin, `f(x) = ±1`.
Ringed points are **support vectors**. **Drag any point.** Move one that isn't ringed and nothing
happens — the boundary ignores it completely. Move a ringed one and the whole solution shifts.
Click empty space to add a point of the selected class.

## The widest street

Write the boundary as `w · x + b = 0` and scale `w` so the closest points of each class sit at
`w · x + b = ±1`. The gap between those two lines is `2 / ‖w‖` wide. Maximizing the margin is
therefore the same as:

```
minimize  ½‖w‖²     subject to  yᵢ (w · xᵢ + b) ≥ 1   for every i
```

This is a convex quadratic program, so it has one answer and no local optima. On the
**Separable** data with a linear kernel, the *Margin width* tile is that `2 / ‖w‖`, in plot units.

## Only the support vectors matter

Solved in its **dual** form, the SVM gives every training point a weight `αᵢ ≥ 0`, and the
boundary is built from them:

```
w = Σ αᵢ yᵢ xᵢ          f(x) = Σ αᵢ yᵢ K(xᵢ, x) + b
```

For almost every point, `αᵢ` comes out exactly **zero**. The bar chart shows it: a few tall bars,
then nothing. The points with `αᵢ > 0` are the support vectors, the ones on or inside the margin.
You could delete every other training point and get the identical classifier. That's why dragging
an unringed point does nothing: until it crosses into the margin, it isn't part of the solution.

## Soft margins: C

Real data overlaps, and then no line satisfies every constraint. The **soft-margin** SVM lets
points violate the margin at a price:

```
minimize  ½‖w‖² + C · Σ max(0, 1 − yᵢ f(xᵢ))
```

The second term is the **hinge loss**: zero for a point safely outside the margin, growing
linearly as it intrudes. `C` sets the exchange rate between a wide margin and few violations:

- **Small C** means violations are cheap. The margin widens, many points become support vectors
  (the orange rings are points pinned at `α = C`, inside the margin or misclassified), and the
  boundary is smooth and robust to outliers.
- **Large C** means violations are expensive. The margin shrinks to fit every point it can, which
  on noisy data means fitting the noise.

Try *Two blobs*, then sweep C from 0.01 to 1000 and watch the dashed lines close in.

## The kernel trick

Look at the dual formula again: training points appear only inside `K(xᵢ, x)`, which for a
linear SVM is just the dot product `xᵢ · x`. Swap in any other function that behaves like a dot
product in *some* feature space, and the SVM becomes a linear classifier in that space, without
ever computing the features.

- **Polynomial**, `(a · b + 1)²`, is exactly a dot product of the quadratic features
  `(1, x, y, x², y², xy)`, suitably scaled — the same features you can switch on by hand in
  [logistic regression](/visualize/logistic-regression/). Try it on *Circles*.
- **RBF**, `exp(−γ‖a − b‖²)`, corresponds to an *infinite*-dimensional feature space. Each
  support vector contributes a Gaussian bump, and the boundary is where the bumps for the two
  classes balance. **γ** sets the bump width: small γ gives smooth, almost linear boundaries; large
  γ wraps a little island around individual points. Set γ = 30 on *XOR* to watch it memorize.

C and γ interact, so in practice they're tuned together by grid search with cross-validation.

## How it's trained here

The dual is solved exactly with **SMO** (Platt, 1998). It repeatedly picks the pair of `α`s that
most violate the optimality conditions and solves that two-variable problem in closed form. The
pair selection is the second-order rule LIBSVM uses. Each drag retrains from scratch, and the
*SMO iterations* tile counts the pair updates.

## Where SVMs stand

Before deep learning, kernel SVMs held the state of the art on handwritten digits, text
categorization and bioinformatics. They're still excellent for small and medium datasets with
good features. They struggle with scale: the kernel matrix has `n²` entries and training time
grows roughly between `n²` and `n³`. They also don't produce probabilities natively (libraries
add them afterwards, with Platt scaling). On images and text, learned features — CNNs, then
transformers — overtook fixed kernels.
